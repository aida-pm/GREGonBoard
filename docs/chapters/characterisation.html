Characterisation

1 What is a characterisation study?

A characterisation study describes who is in a cohort — their demographics, comorbidities, medications, procedures, and healthcare utilisation at and around the index date. It does not make any causal claims. It answers the question: what does this population look like?

Characterisation studies are used in three distinct contexts in pharmacoepidemiology:

  1. Disease natural history — describe the clinical profile of patients with a condition over time
  2. Treatment utilisation — describe who receives a drug, under what circumstances, and with what co-treatments
  3. Baseline comparability — compare cohorts prior to a causal analysis to assess confounding and guide propensity score specification

Our study: Before running the comparative safety analysis of GLP-1 RA agents, we characterise all five exposure cohorts to understand their baseline profiles and identify potential confounders for acute pancreatitis.

5 3. Large-scale characteristics

Large-scale characterisation reports the prevalence of every condition, drug, and procedure in a specified time window around the index date. This produces potentially thousands of features and is the standard approach in OHDSI network studies.

  summariseLargeScaleCharacteristics(
    window           = list(c(-365, -1), c(0, 0), c(1, 30)),
    eventInWindow    = c("condition_occurrence", "drug_exposure"),
    minimumFrequency = 0.01
  )

NoteThree time windows
  • c(-365, -1) — the year prior to index: captures baseline comorbidities and prior medications (covariates for confounding adjustment)
  • c(0, 0) — the index date itself: captures what was coded at the time of drug initiation (the index event breakdown)
  • c(1, 30) — the 30 days after index: captures early treatment patterns and complications (not used for baseline characterisation, but useful for drug utilisation analyses)

6 4. Comparing cohorts — the SMD

When the goal is to compare two cohorts (as in our head-to-head GLP-1 RA study), we compute the standardised mean difference for each covariate:

#     colour    = "variable_level"
#   )
# smd_result

Our study: For the comparative analysis, we would compare each GLP-1 RA agent against semaglutide as the reference. Any covariate with SMD > 0.1 is a candidate for inclusion in the propensity score model. Important covariates to watch for in the pancreatitis study:

Covariate Why it matters for AP risk
Hypertriglyceridaemia Direct risk factor for AP
Alcohol use disorder Strong AP risk factor
Cholelithiasis (gallstones) Leading cause of AP
Prior pancreatitis Strongest predictor of recurrence
BMI / obesity Metabolic AP risk
Corticosteroid use Drug-induced AP risk

Quick check: You are characterising the five GLP-1 RA cohorts. You find that 28% of exenatide users have a prior record of cholelithiasis, compared to 11% of semaglutide users. What is the SMD approximately, and what should you do?

The SMD for a binary variable is approximately (p1 - p2) / sqrt((p1(1-p1) + p2(1-p2)) / 2). Here: (0.28 - 0.11) / sqrt((0.28×0.72 + 0.11×0.89)/2) ≈ 0.17 / 0.36 ≈ 0.47. This is a large imbalance. Since cholelithiasis (gallstones) is the most common cause of acute pancreatitis worldwide, this is a strong confounder — it is both differentially distributed between the cohorts and directly causes the outcome. It must be included in the propensity score model. Excluding patients with cholelithiasis would change the target population and invalidate generalisation of the results.
