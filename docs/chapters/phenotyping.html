Phenotyping

1 What are phenotypes?

A phenotype in biology refers to the observable characteristics of an organism, as opposed to the genotype, which is the underlying genetic blueprint. In the context of observational health data, the term has been adopted with an analogous meaning: a phenotype is a specification of an observable, potentially changing clinical state of a patient, inferred from EHR data, claims data, or other routinely collected health data.

In practice, when we talk about phenotyping in an OMOP study, we mean the process of algorithmically identifying patients who have a particular condition, who received a particular treatment, or who experienced a particular outcome. This is done based on the diagnostic codes, prescriptions, measurements, and clinical notes recorded in their data.

Caution

A phenotype is not a direct observation of clinical reality, but a computational inference about clinical reality based on whatever was recorded in the database!

This distinction matters. A patient coded as having type 2 diabetes mellitus in an EHR may have been coded that way because a clinician confirmed the diagnosis after laboratory testing, because a coding clerk assigned a default code, or because the diagnosis was carried forward from a previous admission without review. The code is the same in all three cases but the clinical reality is not. Phenotyping is the discipline of navigating this uncertainty systematically. In OMOP studies, phenotyping applies to all three core building blocks of a cohort study: the exposure (who received the drug?), the outcome (who experienced the event?), and the population (who has the condition of interest?). You will need to consider all of these elements when building the phenotype for your population of interest, and it is essential that you consider all the domains in which the exposure or outcome that you want to describe might be captured in a relevant way for your study. Do you want to include the concept hyperglycemia in your diabetes study? Then, you might need conditions (hyperglycemia due to type 2 diabetes mellitus) as well as measurements (blood glucose) or observations.

Important

Remember: In the feasibility stage, you will need to provide up to 5 phenotypes. You can ask for help to WP6 in case you need it, both for clinical and programming assistance, since you will need to think of the clinical, sociodemographic, etc. characteristics that your population might have, as well as how to implement it with ATLAS (see the ATLAS section) or CohortConstructor (see the Cohort construction section).

2 Evaluating a phenotype

When we evaluate a phenotype algorithm, we are essentially asking: of all the patients in the database who truly have the condition, how many does our algorithm correctly identify (sensitivity)? And of all the patients our algorithm flags, how many truly have the condition (positive predictive value / specificity)?

Note

Sensitivity measures how well a test correctly identifies people who have a condition, while specificity measures how well a test correctly identifies people who do not have it.

These two properties pull in opposite directions, and the right trade-off depends on the study type:

Study type Priority Rationale
Incidence / prevalence High sensitivity Better to capture all cases, even if some are false positives
Safety outcome (rare event) High specificity A false positive outcome event can create a spurious safety signal
Exposure definition High specificity Misclassifying non-users as users is a major source of confounding
Characterisation Balanced Describes the cohort as captured; misclassification affects interpretation

Let’s go back to our study example. Why does specificity matter more for the outcome? Acute pancreatitis is a serious adverse event with a relatively low background incidence (~2-5 per 1,000 person-years in T2DM patients). A low-specificity outcome phenotype, that is, a phenotype that captures chronic pancreatitis follow-up visits, pancreatitis history codes, or rule-out diagnoses coded as confirmatory, would inflate the event count and potentially dilute or distort the hazard ratio. For this reason, restricting the outcome to inpatient or emergency settings is a deliberate specificity-improving decision, even at the cost of some sensitivity.

Quick check: You are designing the outcome phenotype for acute pancreatitis. You have two options: (A) any condition occurrence record for acute pancreatitis in any care setting, or (B) at least one inpatient or emergency acute pancreatitis record. Which has higher specificity?

Option B has higher specificity because restricting to inpatient or emergency settings filters out the codes most likely to represent historical diagnoses, follow-up visits for previously diagnosed pancreatitis, or diagnostic uncertainty (where a clinician codes a rule-out condition). Nevertheless, outpatient acute pancreatitis codes are far more likely to be false positives than inpatient codes, where the diagnosis typically requires clinical examination, laboratory findings, and often imaging confirmation. The trade-off is reduced sensitivity: some true acute pancreatitis events managed in outpatient settings will be missed; but for a rare serious adverse event, this trade-off is generally acceptable.

3 Tools for phenotyping and evaluating phenotypes

The OHDSI ecosystem provides a structured, layered set of tools that cover the full phenotyping workflow: from building concept sets and cohort definitions, to diagnosing their performance, to formally evaluating their sensitivity and specificity, to sharing validated definitions with the global community.

3.1 The OHDSI Phenotype Library

The OHDSI Phenotype Library is the community’s shared repository of cohort definitions, subject to version control, open peer review, and regular quality checks across the OHDSI network. Each entry includes not only the cohort definition JSON (from ATLAS) but also clinical descriptions, evaluation results from CohortDiagnostics and PheValuator, contributor attribution, and a stable DOI that can be cited in publications.

3.2 Building the phenotype: ATLAS/CodelistGenerator+CohortConstructor

ATLAS is a web tool where you can build your concept sets and cohort definitions. The cohort definition is the phenotype algorithm in its executable form (a JSON specification that can be run against any OMOP CDM database). If you are using R instead of ATLAS, you can use the packages CodelistGenerator and CohortConstructor, which will be explained in the following sections. The executable form is then the R script itself since you create the cohorts in your OMOP CDM database, and you do not need the JSON file.

The key decisions at this stage are:

  • Which concept(s) to use and whether to include descendants
  • Which OMOP domain(s) to search (condition occurrence, drug exposure, measurement, etc.)
  • What temporal logic to apply (first ever occurrence, any occurrence, occurrence within a window)
  • What additional inclusion and exclusion criteria to apply
Tip

Before designing a phenotype from scratch, always search the OHDSI Phenotype Library! It is a publicly accessible, version-controlled catalogue of peer-reviewed cohort definitions contributed by the OHDSI community. Each entry includes clinical descriptions, technical logic, evaluation results, and a stable DOI.

This section is further developed in the ATLAS, CodelistGenerator and CohortConstructor chapters.

3.3 Diagnosing the phenotype: CohortDiagnostics/PhenotypeR

CohortDiagnostics and PhenotypeR are R packages that produce a standardised battery of diagnostics for any cohort definition, without sharing patient-level data. It is the essential next step after building a cohort and before running any analysis. They help ascertain whether you have created your cohort properly or you might be missing something (or someone).

This section is further developed in the Diagnostics chapter.

3.4 Evaluating sensitivity and specificity with PheValuator

PheValuator is an R package that provides a semi-automated method for estimating the sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value (PPV) of a phenotype algorithm without requiring manual chart review. It does this by building a diagnostic prediction model using a cohort of patients very likely to be true positives (the xSpec — extremely specific cohort — defined by requiring multiple diagnostic codes over time) and applying it to a large random sample of the database to generate a predicted probability of the condition for every patient. These probabilities are then used as a probabilistic gold standard against which the rule-based phenotype algorithm is evaluated.

The key outputs of PheValuator are:

  • Sensitivity: the proportion of true cases captured by the phenotype algorithm
  • Specificity: the proportion of true non-cases correctly excluded
  • PPV: the proportion of algorithm-identified cases that are true cases
  • NPV: the proportion of algorithm-excluded patients that are true non-cases
Note

This is a nice addition to the phenotyping process, but it is not a required step.
